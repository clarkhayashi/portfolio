## Production prompt

Create a clear, friendly explainer video for Bad Bets, a casual browser party game for 2–8 friends. The viewer wants to start playing, not hear a product pitch. Aim for about 2–3 minutes. Use a single relaxed American-English narrator if the format supports it. If there are two voices, use one to explain and the other only for short, useful questions. Do not stage a fake podcast conversation.

Sound like a friend explaining a game while everyone gets settled. Use contractions, varied sentence lengths and natural pauses. Read “Bad Bets” normally, not as an acronym. Avoid announcer energy, exaggerated enthusiasm, corporate jargon, dramatic emphasis on every sentence, filler laughter and slogans. Never say “dive in,” “ultimate,” “game changer,” “unleash,” or “a whole game night, one room code.” Do not promise a human voice; simply aim for natural, intelligible delivery. Keep music absent or very quiet behind speech. No alcohol or real-money wagering instructions.

Use simple, readable visuals: warm off-white background, dark navy text, teal for navigation. Give each game its own accent color. Use large labels, phones, room-code examples and clear chip arithmetic. Avoid grids of tiny cards, busy stock footage, generic AI characters and Nintendo/Jackbox assets. Captions should be accurate and easy to read on a phone. Show one idea at a time. Use actual screenshots when supplied; otherwise create clear illustrative diagrams, not invented app screens.

Follow this sequence. Keep the exact rules below accurate, while phrasing the narration conversationally.

### 1. Join and choose a mode — about 20 seconds
Visual: one person selects Start a game; friends select Join a game and enter example room code ABCD. Label it “Example code.”
Suggested narration: “One person starts a game. Everyone else opens the same site on their phone and joins with the room code. Pick Party mode if you want chips and a winner at the end. Or choose Minigames to pick something and play without betting.”
No account or install required. Do not claim remote physical games work: Shadowbox and Keep It Going require everyone together in person. Other games do not include built-in voice chat.

### 2. Set up the party — about 15 seconds
Visual: round choices 3, 6, 9, 12, 15; selected games; host presses Start.
Suggested narration: “The host chooses the games and how many rounds to play: three, six, nine, twelve or fifteen. Nine is the default. Everyone starts with one hundred pretend chips. Most chips at the end wins.”
Do not say every session is nine rounds. Minigames has no tournament round limit or chip scoring.

### 3. Explain a regular round — about 25 seconds
Visual: choose 5, 10 or 20; game revealed; chip example using three separate outcomes.
Suggested narration: “Most rounds include everyone. Before the game is picked, choose five, ten or twenty chips. Say you have a hundred and bet ten. Win, and you finish with a hundred ten. Lose, and you have ninety. If the round is a draw, you get your bet back.”
On-screen math: 100 → win 110 / loss 90 / draw 100. Those are final balances, not payouts to add again. Bets are held during the round. Five is the default if no selection is made. Do not describe a shared poker pot, cash prize or purchasable chips.

### 4. Explain spotlight and audience — about 30 seconds
Visual: every third round highlighted; entry 5 → 10 → 20; two contestants plus audience.
Suggested narration: “Every third round is a spotlight round. Choose to play as the entry amount rises, or step into the audience for free. Only the players selected for the matchup pay the entry. The audience still has a job: judge the creative rounds, predict a team in Ballpark, or help keep score in Shadowbox.”
Fairness line: “If you're judging the winner, you can't bet on that result.”
Ballpark audience predictions cost five chips: correct gains five net, incorrect loses five, team draw refunds. These are optional. No prediction betting on subjective creative or physical games. A player who doesn't answer the entry prompt joins the audience. If fewer than two volunteer, an eligible free everyone-plays game is used, or the round skips if none is available. Keep this fallback as an optional brief caption, not a distracting spoken detour.

### 5. Show the seven games — about 35 seconds
Use short demonstrations rather than reading a dense list.
- Same Brain?: answer a prompt and try to match somebody. Use an illustrative prompt and two “pizza” answers. It matches normalized text, not every synonym.
- Ballpark: guess a number. Closest wins; spotlight teams use average error.
- Food Court Draft: two players draft a meal, pitch it, and the audience votes. This is not an auction.
- Drawn Into Trouble: draw an absurd prompt; the audience chooses a winner. Do not show a caption-writing feature, because it is not implemented.
- Who’s Faking?: everyone gets a category; one player does not get the secret word. Give clues in the displayed order, then discuss and vote.
- Shadowbox: face each other; point or look on the high beep. Same direction means a hit. A dodge switches the attacker. First to three total hits wins, not three consecutive hits. Use the host's sound and confirm scores afterward.
- Keep It Going: choose any category together, or let the game pick. Clap twice, then say a new item on the high beep. Two laps; fewest mistakes wins. Examples: US states, fast-food chains, pizza toppings. The group judges mistakes.
Mention that the menu shows which games fit the number of players; not every game supports two players.

### 6. Finish with what to do next — about 15 seconds
Visual: result, Next round, final standings, Start a game.
Suggested narration: “Check the result, then the host moves the game on. Run low on chips? You'll start the next round with at least five, so nobody gets knocked out. When the chosen rounds are done, the highest balance wins. Tied leaders share the win. Grab your phones and start a game.”
Optional setup caption: “With game bans enabled, everyone can vote to remove one option before each round, when at least three are eligible.” Do not imply that ban voting is mandatory.

## Accuracy checklist before export

- Show 3/6/9/12/15 selectable rounds; nine is only the default.
- Betting is pretend chips; no payments, prizes, alcohol or elimination.
- 100 with a 10-chip bet ends at 110/90/100.
- Spotlight happens every third round. Stepping out is free; selected contestants pay.
- Judges cannot bet on the outcome they decide.
- Seven games; two public modes. Mixer is not in the public menu.
- Physical games use host-device audio and in-person play.
- No auction, drawing titles, automated voice detection, accounts or built-in voice chat claims.
- End with starting/joining, not an advertisement or long recap.
